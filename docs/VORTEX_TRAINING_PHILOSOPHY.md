# Vortex Athletics Training Philosophy

> Coaching reference · Merged September 12, 2026

**Prepare well. Move explosively. Get strong. Control your body.**

Vortex develops capable, confident young athletes who can produce, absorb and redirect force, move with purpose, and control their bodies in space. We combine broad athletic development, progressive strength and dedicated tumbling with enjoyable practice that athletes can sustain for years.

Purpose over novelty. Quality over fatigue. Competency before progression. Progress measured against the athlete’s own starting point.

## Four development components

1. **Prepare & Access:** Get warm, access useful positions, coordinate movement and rehearse today’s demands without fatigue.
2. **Explosiveness:** Sprint, jump, throw, brake, redirect and react while intent and movement quality are high.
3. **Strength:** Build progressive full-body force production, usable range and the capacity to tolerate athletic loading.
4. **Body Control:** Develop balance, orientation, rotation, inversion and confident landings through coached tumbling progressions.

## Keep all eight tenets

The Athleticism Accelerator’s eight tenets remain the outcomes we develop. The four components organize time; methodologies describe how we practice and load movement; physiology explains the intended adaptation. A tenet does not need its own time block.

| Tenet | What we build | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strength | Produce and resist force. | Full-body lifting, calisthenics, carries and support positions. |
| Explosiveness | Express force quickly. | Quality jumps, throws, bounds and explosive starts. |
| Speed | Move rapidly with effective mechanics. | Acceleration and upright sprinting with sufficient space and recovery. |
| Agility | Brake, redirect and respond to relevant information. | Planned cuts progressing to partner or visual-cue decisions. |
| Flexibility/Mobility | Access useful range and control it. | Dynamic preparation, controlled strength ranges and appropriate later stretching. |
| Balance | Maintain or regain stability. | Landing sticks, unilateral work, travel and tumbling. |
| Coordination | Organize rhythm and whole-body timing. | Skipping, crawling, throwing and linked movement. |
| Body Control | Know and control the body’s position in space. | Shapes, rolls, support, inversion, rotation and landings. |

- Review tenet coverage across an athlete’s actual attendance. Every session offers broad development, with one or two qualities receiving a larger emphasis. All eight do not need equal doses every day.
- An exercise has one primary job and may support several other tenets. Multiple tags do not multiply its training dose or prove that every quality received enough practice.

## A session clock coaches can deliver

Use Prepare & Access → Explosiveness → Strength → Body Control as the default developmental sequence. The standard experience is 90 minutes of athletic work plus 30 minutes of separately programmed tumbling. The compressed experience is 60 plus 30: 90 minutes total. All allocations include explanation, recovery, water and transitions.

| Component | Standard: 120 min total | Compressed: 90 min total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prepare & Access | 0–15 · 15 min | 0–10 · 10 min |
| Explosiveness | 15–45 · 30 min | 10–35 · 25 min |
| Strength | 45–75 · 30 min | 35–55 · 20 min |
| Flexible window | 75–90 · 15 min | 55–60 · 5 min |
| Body Control / Tumbling | 90–120 · 30 min | 60–90 · 30 min |

- The flexible window is optional Capacity / Competition, extra recovery, coaching or a tumbling readiness reset. It is not a fifth required pillar. If tumbling follows, use low-fatigue play or preparation; omit hard conditioning. Competition can also be a few fully recovered attempts inside Explosiveness.
- When behind schedule, remove the optional finisher first, then secondary drills and sets. Preserve preparation, recovery between quality efforts, core full-body strength and the scheduled Body Control block. Fewer well-coached exercises fit better than faster rotations.
- A 30-minute Explosiveness allocation includes demonstration and generous rest. It never means 30 minutes of continuous sprinting or jumping. Teach a new warm-up over several visits instead of adding teaching time outside the clock.
- If only 60 minutes TOTAL are available, use 8 Prepare & Access + 15 Explosiveness + 17 Strength + 20 Body Control, with no finisher. Do not advertise a 60-minute athletic block as a complete 60-minute class when 30 minutes of tumbling follow.
- Existing shared-floor logistics may place tumbling first for one group. Treat this as a scheduling exception: prepare within the tumbling allocation and complete athletic preparation again before speed work. Reassess fatigue; do not add missed volume or maximal output to compensate.

## Prepare & Access: familiar base, specific finish

Retain the Vortex preparation framework: Raise → Mobilize → Activate → Integrate → Potentiate Bridge. These are preparation purposes, not five extra timed blocks. Several purposes can occur in the same drill. A predictable routine reduces explanation and lets coaches notice readiness changes.

| Order | Full base routine | Starting dose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Easy jog | 60 seconds |
| 2 | Backpedal | 10 m |
| 3 | Lateral shuffle | 10 m each direction |
| 4 | Carioca | 10 m each direction |
| 5 | Walking lunge with rotation/reach | 5 m |
| 6 | Lateral lunge with reach | 5 m |
| 7 | Inchworm → plank → bear crawl → downward dog | Up to 10 m; shorten the sequence as needed |
| 8 | Traveling snap-down to athletic stick | A few controlled repetitions over 10 m |
| 9–12 | A-march, A-skip, B-skip, C-skip | 10 m each; use mastered variations |
| 13 | Ankling | 10 m |
| 14 | Low two-foot pogos | Up to 10 quiet, controlled contacts |
| 15 | Bodyweight hip hinge with reach | 5 repetitions |
| 16 | Squat-to-stand with overhead reach | 5 comfortable repetitions |

- Use the sequence below as the familiar full routine in the 15-minute allocation. Distances are short teaching lanes, not quotas. Scale travel, repetitions and complexity to the group. Athletes should finish warm, organized and ready to move quickly.
- In 10 minutes, use about 7 minutes for the base: easy jog; backpedal and shuffle; walking and lateral lunges; one crawl sequence; snap-down; A-march or A-skip; ankling or low pogos; hinge and squat checks. Use the remaining 3 minutes for two daily-specific rehearsals. Rotate carioca and B/C-skips into suitable sessions instead of rushing all 16 drills.
- Finish the base with a bodyweight hinge and squat so the coach can observe foot pressure, hip and knee control, trunk position and comfortable range. Cue hips back for the hinge and a stable trunk in both; do not force depth, knee position or an exaggerated back arch.
- Then use exactly two purposeful preparation tasks: one position/mechanics rehearsal and one progressive version of today’s explosive task. Acceleration: start position → progressive starts. Cutting: controlled brake/plant → quicker cut. Jumping: small jump-and-stick → purposeful practice jump. Throwing: light pattern → progressive throw. Upright sprinting: rhythm rehearsal → buildup.
- Replace a drill that is painful, too complex or mismatched to today’s work. Preparation is observation and practice, not a medical screen or a guarantee against injury. Do not exhaust stabilizers or count warm-up hops as a reason to add more impact later.

## Explosiveness: protect the quality of the effort

Train acceleration, upright speed, jumping, throwing, braking, change of direction and reactive agility deliberately. Choose one primary objective and, when useful, one complementary objective. Maximal intent means the best appropriate effort for that athlete’s current skill.

- Begin with a predictable task and a safe stopping area. Teach force absorption before adding approach speed, repeated rebounds or sharper cuts. Add perception and decisions only after the basic task is controlled.
- A coach-selected starting example is 4–6 accelerations over 10–20 m, or 1–3 sets of 3–5 quality jumps or throws. These are alternatives and adjustable starting points, not a daily minimum or an instruction to do every category.
- Allow about 60–120 seconds between short speed efforts; longer or more demanding efforts may need 2–3 minutes or more. Begin the next effort only when breathing, focus and mechanics are ready. A relay becomes conditioning when waiting time is too short to preserve speed.
- Count jump and landing exposure across warm-up, explosive work, games, tumbling and outside sport. Begin with a modest dose and adjust to the athlete; do not impose one contact target across all ages and impact levels.
- For upright speed, provide enough buildup, running space, run-off and supervision. A short indoor shuttle is not a substitute for maximal-velocity sprinting. Choose short acceleration or throwing when space does not support the intended task.
- End a set when landing, mechanics or intent deteriorate. Rest and retry an easier version; if quality remains below the athlete’s normal level, stop that stimulus. Timings and distances inform coaching, but noisy measurements do not justify a universal percentage cutoff.
- A useful integrated example is broad jump → stick → directional cue → short acceleration. Use it only when each part is mastered; a simple sprint or jump remains valuable without added decisions.

## Strength: full-body exposure, progressive loading

Each session includes lower-body work, pushing, pulling and trunk control at an appropriate dose. Rotate the emphasis among squat, hinge, lunge/step, push, pull and carry/brace patterns so an athlete attending once is still served. The clock limits how much each pattern receives.

- Use a main lower-body pattern plus a small dose of a complementary lower-body pattern, one push and one pull. A carry, supported position or brace can provide trunk work. Pair exercises that use different regions when that preserves rest and coach visibility.
- For a new athlete, a practical starting point is 1–2 sets of 8–12 controlled repetitions on selected strength exercises, with light resistance. Experienced athletes may use other appropriate rep ranges or an additional set. Stop while a few technically sound repetitions remain; no forced reps, grinding lifts or routine failure.
- Rest roughly 60–90 seconds for moderate work and longer when load, attention or technique requires it. Time spent at a different station is not automatically recovery if it fatigues the same muscles.
- Earn load through repeatable technique and comfortable range. Increase one variable at a time using the smallest practical load increase; do not require true one-repetition maximum testing. Appropriately sized weights are an option when readiness, instruction and equipment fit.
- Embed resilience through controlled lowering, brief isometrics, unilateral work and appropriate calf/foot, hamstring, adductor and shoulder support. Use a targeted drill for an identified need, not a compulsory extra circuit. Heavy eccentric overload, overspeed assistance and other specialized methods are not default youth programming.
- The 20-minute example is 2 minutes of setup, 8 minutes alternating a squat and row, 8 minutes alternating a hinge and incline push-up, and 2 minutes for a brief carry/brace and handoff. Use short sets with rest, regressions and only one main loading emphasis. The 30-minute version adds coaching, recovery or a quality set rather than more stations by default.

## Body Control: protected practice through tumbling

Tumbling is a dedicated developmental component. It builds orientation, balance, rotation, inversion, upper-body support, landings and confidence. Progress toward useful body control whether or not the athlete pursues competitive gymnastics.

- Progress from shapes, floor support and balance to appropriate rolls, inversion, rotation and linked skills. Prerequisites are skill-specific: age, bravery or one successful attempt does not establish readiness for a harder skill.
- Plan the 30 minutes as approximately 5 minutes of readiness checks and specific wrist/shoulder/landing preparation, 20 minutes of coached skill practice with rest, and 5 minutes of familiar consolidation and downshift. Teach one skill family with individual progressions.
- Before every progression, check attention, support strength, orientation and landing control. Following athletic work, choose familiar foundations when fatigue is present. A water break does not restore full readiness for advanced tumbling.
- New, high-consequence or advanced skills require fresh, supervised practice. When the default end-of-session placement cannot provide that, use foundations today and place advanced learning in a separately planned fresh session. Never press ahead to complete the written plan.
- Use appropriate mats, clear travel lanes, qualified coaching and spotting where required. Offer an accessible floor-level variation for athletes who cannot yet tolerate inversion or rotation. Pain, dizziness, distress or loss of control ends the attempt and triggers the facility’s response procedure as appropriate.

## Movement intelligence and resilience run throughout

Movement intelligence is organizing the body, perceiving relevant information and adapting movement to a task. Resilience is developing the ability to tolerate, absorb, control and recover from athletic loading. Both guide exercise selection across the four components.

- Prepare & Access: rhythm, contralateral crawling, usable range and landing positions. Explosiveness: force production, braking, jump-and-stick, planned cuts and appropriate reactions.
- Strength: unilateral control, carries, controlled lowering and stable force production. Body Control: orientation, support, rotation and landings. Record where each intended stimulus occurred before adding anything else.
- Use balance in context: stabilize after a landing, control a split squat, travel across a suitable surface. Static balance remains useful for an individual need. Keep loaded force-production tasks stable rather than adding unstable surfaces indiscriminately.
- Ladders and memorized patterns can teach rhythm and coordination. Reactive agility also requires a meaningful cue and a movement choice; it cannot be replaced by ladder speed.
- Keep methodological language honest: a tenet is an outcome, a training family supplies movements, a method changes loading or execution, and a protocol organizes work/rest. Do not select drills because they carry the most labels.

## One to five visits: complement, recover, repeat

Every visit stands alone; a series of visits also progresses. Plan a useful baseline exposure plus the day’s emphasis. Attendance is not a prescription for five hard training days. Sport practice, competition, school activity, soreness and sleep all affect the dose.

| Example day | Explosive emphasis | Strength emphasis; retain full-body exposure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Acceleration + horizontal jump-and-stick | Squat emphasis; light hinge, push, pull and brace |
| Tuesday | Lower-impact throws + rhythm or simple reaction | Technique dose for returning athletes; unilateral pattern practice |
| Wednesday | Upright speed with space, or short acceleration | Hinge emphasis; complementary knee pattern, push and pull |
| Thursday | Controlled braking + planned-to-reactive direction change | Light movement practice and carrying for frequent attendees |
| Friday | Selected jump/throw quality + recovered competitive attempts | Full-body development if recovered; otherwise technique and review |

- One visit: deliver all four components and full-body strength exposure; do not try to make up a week of volume. Two or three visits: distribute meaningful strength and explosive loading with recovery between demanding exposures.
- Four or five visits: alternate development days with lighter technique, low-impact movement, mobility, easy aerobic play and familiar body-control practice. The same session theme can have a reduced-load version for an athlete returning tomorrow or recovering from yesterday.
- For beginners, plan substantive resistance training on about 2–3 nonconsecutive days when attendance allows. On adjacent visits, strength exposure may mean easy pattern practice rather than another challenging load. Do not repeat heavy eccentric work or high-impact plyometrics simply because the calendar changes.
- Track each athlete’s recent loading and actual attendance. An athlete who missed the prerequisite session starts at the appropriate regression today. Persistent soreness, reduced enthusiasm or repeated quality decline calls for less training stress and a conversation, not punishment.
- Rotate acceleration, upright speed where space permits, jumps/throws, braking/cutting and reaction across the program. Over several weeks, check that irregular attendees receive the important movement families and all eight tenets; daily baseline exposure alone does not guarantee balanced long-term coverage.

## Control → Produce → Redirect → React

This is a teaching progression, not a rigid age ladder. An athlete may be advanced in strength and beginning in rotation. Acquire the pattern, develop it through repeatable practice, apply it at useful output, then integrate appropriate decisions.

- Control: own a position, useful range, landing or stop. Produce: increase force or speed with that control intact. Redirect: absorb and change direction. React: respond to a cue without losing the underlying skill.
- Progress when the athlete can repeat the task with consistent control, understand a simple cue and recover appropriately across sessions. Make one change: load, range, speed, approach distance, repetitions or decision demand. Better execution is itself progress.
- Keep foundational exercises for a coherent block, often 4–6 weeks, while adjusting the dose and small variations. Review readiness each visit; reduce load or complexity during growth-related coordination changes, after absence or when quality falls.
- Use age bands to organize communication and equipment, with training age, maturity, attention and demonstrated competency deciding the prescription. Offer a regression, foundation and progression within the same station.
- Reward effort, technique, curiosity, cooperation and consistency. Explain one useful cue, demonstrate, then let athletes practice. Use inclusive games and personal challenges; exercise is never punishment and body size is not a performance grade.

## Capacity, competition and recovery with a purpose

Capacity is the ability to repeat useful work. Competition adds engagement and decision-making when its format supports the objective. Neither requires exhaustion. Build a durable relationship with movement alongside performance.

- Use short sled efforts, carries, relays, tag, med-ball games or easy work/rest circuits when space, supervision, recovery and the remaining session support them. Select the intensity for the intended adaptation.
- Before tumbling, keep the flexible window low fatigue or devote it to recovery and skill preparation. A demanding conditioning session belongs after all sensitive skill work in a separately planned format; do not move it there by extending the booked session.
- Integrate a brief downshift, comfortable mobility and reflection into the final minutes. Encourage adequate sleep, hydration, regular meals, free play and recovery from organized sport. No finishers to compensate for missed sessions.

## The coach’s planning and review standard

A strong session has a clear objective, a teachable dose, recoverable loading and a place in the athlete’s longer development. The plan should be simple enough to run well with the available coaches, space and equipment.

- Before planning: identify the group, readiness, restrictions, attendance history, other sport demands and the actual booked time. Choose the explosive objective and complementary full-body strength emphasis, then the familiar preparation and two specific rehearsals.
- Before delivery: name the body-control skill family and prerequisites; locate movement intelligence and resilience already embedded; decide whether the optional window adds value. Assign safe lanes, equipment, coach positions and clear station instructions before athletes arrive.
- For each exercise record the primary purpose, sets/reps or distance/time, rest, load guidance, regression, progression and stop rule. Include impact, complexity, space and supervision requirements. Budget setup and transitions; avoid long queues or too many tasks for one coach to observe.
- During delivery: adjust the dose for the athlete who attends once and the one who trained yesterday. Protect quality and stop or modify for pain, dizziness, distress or materially altered mechanics. Follow medical restrictions and facility emergency procedures.
- After delivery: record actual attendance, completed dose, important impacts, readiness, skill competency and athlete feedback. Every 4–6 weeks review a small repeatable set of measures such as a short sprint, jump-and-stick, a controlled strength pattern and a body-control skill. Compare the athlete to their own baseline using consistent conditions.
- Use performance and enjoyment together: improved control, appropriate strength, quality output, confident participation and willingness to return. No single score, soreness level or calorie count defines a successful youth session.

## Evidence and coaching judgment

The session clock, drill selections and examples are Vortex coaching decisions informed by the merged philosophy; they are adjustable starting points, not research-established optimal doses for every child. The sources below support the general youth-development principles.

- [American Academy of Pediatrics: Resistance Training for Children and Adolescents (2020; reaffirmed 2024)](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/145/6/e20201011/76942/Resistance-Training-for-Children-and-Adolescents): Supports supervised, technique-led youth resistance training and progression matched to the child.
- [NSCA: Youth Resistance Training position statement (2009)](https://www.nsca.com/globalassets/about/position-statements/position_stand_youth_resistance_training---2009.pdf): Supports conservative starting doses, gradual progression and recovery between substantive strength sessions.
- [NSCA: Youth Training and Long-Term Athletic Development](https://www.nsca.com/about-us/position-statements/youth-training-and-long-term-athletic-development/): Supports broad physical literacy, foundational strength and development for children of differing abilities.

## Relationship to existing Vortex programming

This merged philosophy preserves the eight Athleticism Accelerator tenets and Vortex’s Raise → Mobilize → Activate → Integrate → Potentiate Bridge preparation framework. It replaces seven mandatory time blocks with four developmental components, embeds movement intelligence and resilience, and makes conditioning optional.

Existing library keys remain available: prepare_and_access maps to Prepare & Access; output describes explosive work; capacity describes strength work; movement_intelligence and resilience identify qualities embedded across components; sustained_capacity identifies optional conditioning; restore identifies the downshift. Body Control / Tumbling remains separately programmed. These tags are not extra required minutes.

This is the coaching standard and an update to the philosophy views. It does not migrate saved workouts, change the existing Flip & Fit schedule, alter exercise IDs, or implement new generator validation. Older session templates and automated rules remain software references and require explicit reconciliation before they can be described as enforcing this philosophy.
