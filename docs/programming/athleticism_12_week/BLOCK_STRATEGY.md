# Vortex continuing athleticism block

**Stage 1: philosophy and block strategy · Independently reviewed; operating assumptions remain explicit**  
**Scope:** a recurring 12-week offering of 60 athletic sessions, plus a separate four-week offering of 20 instructional sessions. No calendar dates are assigned. No detailed sessions are prescribed in this stage.

**Facility update — September 13, 2026:** The owner confirms **three 12 m running lanes** and the available equipment listed in the shared [facility and equipment reference](../FACILITY_AND_EQUIPMENT.md). Apply that reference to past-program revisions and all future planning. Earlier 15–35 m running layouts require revision; their prior written checks do not establish a fit to the confirmed facility.

## The purpose of a continuing block

A Vortex block is a period of directed development within an athlete’s continuing training. It begins from the athlete’s recent completed work, retains useful skills and exercise anchors, develops selected qualities, and hands a documented finishing point to the next block. It is not a course that returns every athlete to beginner instruction every three months.

The eight tenets remain **Strength, Explosiveness, Speed, Agility, Flexibility/Mobility, Balance, Coordination and Body Control**. The delivery sequence remains **Prepare & Access → Explosiveness → Strength → separate Body Control / Tumbling**, with an appropriate Capacity / Competition or readiness-transition window inside the athletic booking. Movement intelligence and resilience are developed within those components.

**Ages 12–14 identify the reference prescription, not the beginner level.** A competent 12-year-old begins with productive training at an established level. A new 17-year-old uses the instruction or regression needed for that particular task. Control → Produce → Redirect → React describes available progression decisions for an athlete and movement; it does not assign the entire group to four successive remedial stages.

This competency-led approach is consistent with the distinction between training age and skill competency in the [AAP resistance-training report](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/145/6/e20201011/76942/Resistance-Training-for-Children-and-Adolescents). The recurring-block structure below is Vortex’s proposed application, not a protocol established by that report.

Week 1 includes brief calibration within actual training. It confirms the athlete’s current variation, usable range, effort tolerance, recovery and skill prerequisites. It does not defer sprinting or meaningful strength work until a later month. Missing records justify a conservative calibration of the affected movement, not a blanket assumption of novice status.

## What should improve, and what should stay available

The block emphasizes repeatable acceleration and explosive output, a stronger full-body force foundation, and controlled braking and redirection that can support appropriate reactions. Continued tumbling develops spatial control through its own approved progression. The final daily outline will make the emphasis of each session specific without pretending every supporting exercise causes the primary adaptation.

| Outcome | How improvement will be recognized | What is retained when another quality leads |
|---|---|---|
| Speed and explosive output | Comparable short sprint, jump or throw observations under recorded conditions; equal or better output with sound mechanics and adequate recovery. Timing variation is interpreted in context. | Familiar acceleration, jump and throw exposures; upright speed only where space and run-off permit it. |
| Strength | More controlled work or a more demanding appropriate load at the same usable range, technique and effort target; or the same work performed with better control. No routine maximal strength test. | Lower-body knee and hip patterns, push, pull, unilateral control and carry/brace exposure across actual attendance. |
| Braking, redirection and agility | An athlete can stop or redirect from the prescribed approach, remain balanced, and respond to a relevant cue without losing the established movement quality. | Predictable braking and landing practice even when reaction becomes an emphasis. |
| Mobility, balance and coordination | Comfortable task-specific range, controlled single-support/landing positions and reliable whole-body timing during real tasks. | The familiar preparation sequence, useful loaded ranges, rhythm and coordinated movement. |
| Body Control | The tumbling coach records skill-specific shape, support, orientation and landing competency in the separately managed program. | Appropriate familiar body-control practice; difficult skills are not assigned merely to make a phase look more advanced. |
| Sustainable participation | The athlete tolerates the planned exposures, recovers for subsequent work, understands cues and remains willing to participate. | Play, confidence, teamwork and an appropriate relationship with training. |

Targets describe observable performance and competency, not promised percentage gains. Keep comparable test conditions: variation, implement/load, distance, surface, footwear where relevant, timing method, rest and scoring definition. A change of test or equipment needs a new comparison baseline. Reassess a small set of useful measures within training rather than turning every visit into testing.

## Recommended organization: three emphasis periods with four-week review windows

Use three four-week periods as an **operational planning choice**, not a claim that this is the scientifically optimal cycle for every child. It creates understandable review points for coaches and families, accommodates irregular attendance and lets the outline show accumulation, consolidation and carryover. Recovery and individual adjustments also occur inside every week; they are not postponed until the fourth week.

| Period | Purpose and productive work | What changes conditionally | What stays | Review and recovery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–4: Retain and develop the current level | Carry established acceleration, explosive movement and full-body strength forward; calibrate only what is uncertain. Establish repeatable output and stopping conditions for this block. | Usually one chosen variable: execution consistency, usable range, manageable repetitions or appropriate load. Familiar decision tasks may remain for athletes already competent in them. | Useful prior-block anchors, adequate rest, existing skills and meaningful loading from Week 1. | Week 4 is a review/consolidation window. Reduce nonessential sets or demanding contacts when the athlete’s history calls for it; retain quality efforts. No automatic universal percentage reduction. |
| Weeks 5–8: Develop force and purposeful redirection | Continue force production and explosive output while giving selected braking, lateral and reacceleration tasks more attention. | Increase a justified force, speed, approach or redirection demand when the relevant prerequisite is repeatable. Change only the selected variable; a new direction does not also require more load, contacts and density. | Acceleration, jump/throw quality, complementary upper-body work, useful ranges and separately progressed Body Control. | Week 8 reviews response and consolidates. Keep a comparable anchor exposure so a coach can distinguish adaptation from exercise novelty. |
| Weeks 9–12: Apply, verify and carry forward | Express retained force and movement quality in selected output, redirection or perception tasks. Verify useful progress and prepare the next emphasis. | Task specificity or decision demand may rise for ready athletes while total demanding volume is held or reduced. Some athletes improve by repeating the same task more consistently. | Foundational strength, familiar speed exposures, predictable landing/braking practice and recovery. | Week 12 gathers comparable evidence and a handoff. It is not a compulsory peak or maximal test week. Reduce conflicting work around assessment or sport demands; choose the next block from the actual response. |

These are emphasis periods, not exclusive compartments. A competent athlete can sprint, lift, redirect and react in the first period. A developing athlete can continue predictable practice in the final period. An athlete who misses a review week receives an appropriate review at the next relevant visit, without a catch-up workload.

## Consistency, useful variation and the job of a set

Retain a small group of measurable movement or exercise-family anchors across the block and, where productive, across blocks. Candidate anchor families are acceleration, upright sprinting where feasible, jump-and-stick/rebound, throwing, braking/redirection, a knee-dominant lift, a hinge, a unilateral lower-body task, push, pull and carry/brace. This is a candidate family map, not a final exercise selection or an instruction to perform every family at full dose every day. Exact records, variants and programming methods must be resolved from the libraries after the outline audit.

Keep an anchor when the athlete is still learning from it, its purpose remains relevant, and its dose can be adjusted to the surrounding workload. Change it when a documented prerequisite unlocks a useful demand, an outcome requires a different task, the current exercise is no longer productive, or equipment/readiness makes an alternative more appropriate. A different week number is not a reason by itself.

Public performance-coaching material supports retaining a productive lift instead of changing it unnecessarily; its adult/advanced loading scheme is not adopted. See the [public strength article’s variation discussion](https://blog.overtimeathletes.com/absolute-strength-training-method-for-athletes/). Similarly, the [public youth-speed article](https://blog.overtimeathletes.com/youth-speed-training-how-young-athletes-should-be-training-speed/) supports quality sprinting with recovery alongside strength and explosive practice. Vortex retains concise mechanical coaching and rejects that article’s categorical claims about weakness, automatic technique improvement or guaranteed outcomes.

Every later prescription will identify a primary job for each set or group of identical sets: preparation, primary development, complementary development, maintenance, technical practice, assessment or appropriate late-session work. Different jobs are allowed within a coherent full-body session. Upper-body strength can be valuable complementary work without being described as a direct acceleration drill.

First establish the day’s objective and quality target, then the jobs and dose of its work, then the best-fitting exercise variants. Ordering, pairing and rest must support those jobs. A convenient station circuit cannot justify compromising sprint recovery, fatiguing the same tissues repeatedly, or placing unfamiliar tumbling after demanding conditioning.

## Two linked records: the offering and the athlete

The offering calendar has five planned sessions each week. An athlete’s training history contains only the work they actually completed. Progression uses both records, with the athlete’s observed response deciding which eligible prescription to take today.

For each recurring anchor, preserve:

`Prior relevant exposure → current prescription and purpose → next conditional exposure`

Also inspect the adjacent session, because a different exercise family can still create overlapping loading. The last relevant sprint exposure and yesterday’s demanding tumbling both matter, for different reasons.

Each link will record the variation, planned dose, actual completed dose and response when known, what changes or stays, why, and the advance/hold/regress criterion. Unknown actual performance stays unknown. A planned exposure is never recorded as completed, and an attendance gap never becomes accumulated make-up volume.

The next exposure follows a decision, not a calendar command:

- **Ready and meeting the target:** retain or make the one justified change specified for that athlete’s variation.
- **Partly meeting the target:** hold the dose/variation, refine the cue or reduce the limiting demand while preserving the intended job.
- **Fatigued or facing substantial sport load:** remove redundant or optional work first; reduce demanding exposure and retain appropriate familiar practice.
- **Missed relevant exposure:** use the most recent demonstrated level; complete only the calibration or instruction needed today.
- **Pain, dizziness, distress, restriction or materially altered mechanics:** stop or modify as appropriate and follow the existing qualified-professional/facility procedure. A program does not provide medical clearance.

## Flexible attendance without a narrow weekday curriculum

Every visit includes purposeful preparation, an appropriate explosive exposure, full-body strength exposure and the separate body-control interface. Full-body exposure does not mean maximal training of every movement family in every session.

| Attendance | Programming response |
|---|---|
| One visit/week | Give a useful development dose within that visit and use the attendance ledger to rotate emphasis over successive visits. Never cram a week of missed work into one session. |
| Two visits/week | Complement the two completed exposures; avoid using both visits for identical demanding work. Preserve a knee/hip and push/pull balance across the pair while each visit remains broadly useful. |
| Three visits/week | Distribute demanding development exposures with recovery, considering sport and tumbling. Do not assume the visits are nonconsecutive. |
| Four visits/week | Mix development with reduced-demand technical, mobility, rhythm and familiar body-control exposures. A fourth visit is not automatically a fourth hard day. |
| Five visits/week | Make the individual’s sequence recoverable, using lighter practice between demanding exposures and reducing duplicate impacts or substantial strength loading. Total organized sport exposure still governs the adjustment. |

Rotate daily emphasis through the offering slots across weeks so a Monday-only or Thursday-only athlete does not permanently miss an important quality. The outline must audit those fixed-weekday paths as well as consecutive attendance. Objective rotation and loading intensity are separate decisions: a quality can be practiced lightly today or developed more substantially by a recovered infrequent attendee within the same station task.

The detailed outline must show how those individual dose options fit a common coached group. It may not solve irregular attendance by giving everyone five full development doses or by making all infrequent attendees follow the lighter volume intended for frequent attendees.

One weekly visit is useful but is not represented as equivalent to more frequent well-recovered development exposure. The [NSCA youth resistance-training statement](https://www.nsca.com/globalassets/about/position-statements/position_stand_youth_resistance_training---2009.pdf) supports a starting frequency of two or three nonconsecutive substantive strength sessions and adjusting more frequent participation with lighter work. Individual readiness and the approved prescription still decide what the athlete actually does.

Review the whole sport calendar, including weekends. A lighter gym session does not by itself solve an athlete’s lack of time away from organized sport. If overall recovery is inadequate, change attendance or scheduling as well as the training dose. This follows the workload/recovery emphasis in the [AAP overuse and burnout report](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/153/2/e2023065129/196435/Overuse-Injuries-Overtraining-and-Burnout-in-Young); it does not label five visits appropriate for every athlete.

## Entering and leaving a block

Before a continuing athlete starts the next block, retrieve the last relevant productive exposures, recent assessment conditions/results, competency observations, completed attendance, restrictions, coach notes and known sport commitments. Include the latest reduced or assessment exposure, but do not mistake a taper dose for the athlete’s entire established capacity. Nor should one exceptional best attempt become the automatic working prescription.

Retain the appropriate variation and a recoverable known dose when the athlete remains ready. Briefly recalibrate after a gap, growth-related change or uncertain recovery. Select the next emphasis from observed needs and the sport calendar. Carry forward anchors that still serve the purpose; change only those with a documented reason.

**Worked continuity illustration — all data below are hypothetical, not athlete records or finalized prescriptions.** A competent 13-year-old’s prior block includes a familiar goblet squat at 12 kg total external load for 3 × 6 controlled repetitions, approximately three good repetitions in reserve, on two recorded productive exposures. Their last review visit used only 1 × 6 for observation; they also completed four controlled 15 m accelerations with full recovery and satisfactory stopping. No restriction or unusual soreness is reported at the next block’s check.

The new block can begin with the known squat variation and 3 × 6 at the same load, plus the established short-acceleration task at its recent productive dose, with the actual recovery and quality checked again. This is productive continuation, not a month of empty-bar instruction and not an automatic load increase. If the current repetitions are repeatable at the intended effort and the athlete recovers, a later relevant strength exposure might use 3 × 7 at the same load while preserving technique and rest; it may instead remain 3 × 6 if speed or sport load receives the larger emphasis. A change in repetition count needs that stated purpose. If readiness is reduced, the coach lowers the current demand rather than treating last block’s record as permission to push through fatigue. These isolated numbers illustrate carryover only; library eligibility and a complete session are still to be resolved.

The next-block handoff includes the actual response to those choices. Progress is not an obligation to add weight, fatigue or complexity indefinitely.

## The separate instructional on-ramp

The on-ramp is a four-week, five-day **offering** with 20 numbered instructional plans, OR-01 through OR-20. It prepares an athlete to join the current main block at an appropriate variation. Four elapsed calendar weeks neither guarantee competence nor require a competent athlete to repeat mastered instruction.

The instructional map will progress from orientation and controllable tasks, through repeatable practice and appropriate loading, to operating within normal group flow and an observed entry decision. These are teaching priorities; an athlete can move ahead in a mastered domain while receiving targeted work in another. Instruction, demonstration and correction fit inside the existing component clocks by reducing work volume and task complexity, not by silently extending class.

| Entry domain | Observable participation expectation | If the expectation is not met |
|---|---|---|
| Conduct and flow | Respond to stop/start cues, maintain spacing, wait for a clear lane, handle equipment appropriately and report symptoms. | Complete the relevant orientation and supervised practice before independent station participation. |
| Standard preparation | Follow the recognizable sequence at an appropriate drill level, including the concluding hinge and squat checks and the two daily-specific rehearsals. | Teach the missing elements and use explicit easier variants; do not substitute an unrelated warm-up. |
| Sprinting, landing and braking | Organize the chosen start, use the allocated lane and run-off, land or stop under control at the prescribed approach. | Use a slower or simpler supervised variant; do not add approach speed, rebound demand or uncertain reaction. |
| Strength participation | Demonstrate the chosen knee/hip, push/pull and brace variations; understand the coach’s load and rest instructions; stop before technique deteriorates. | Teach and load only the qualified variation. One weak pattern does not require removing productive work from every other pattern. |
| Effort and recovery | Recognize the difference between a quality effort and a fatigue circuit; accept recovery and follow the assigned number of attempts. | Use direct coaching and simple counted efforts/rest before self-managed rotation. |
| Body-control entry | Meet the tumbling coach’s prerequisites for the chosen support, orientation or landing task. | Join the appropriate separate foundational skill lane. Main-program age does not unlock advanced tumbling. |

These are participation domains, not numeric medical screens or approval of advanced skills. Stage 2 will link each domain to the instructional sessions and the main-program demands it unlocks. Stage 3 must use the actual library prerequisites to turn each into an exercise-specific observable criterion, including the within-age beginner/experienced options.

**Different route for a newcomer:** a hypothetical 16-year-old arrives while the group is in Week 6. They demonstrate safe station conduct and familiar push/pull work but lack controlled braking and the required hinge variation. The coach assigns those instructional modules and suitable continuing practice in mastered domains. After the documented checks are met, the athlete joins the main offering current when those checks are met, with the appropriate braking and hinge entry prescriptions. They do not automatically take the group’s progressed dose or restart every established athlete at Day 1. Their first main-block exposure is linked to their actual instructional work; the next change depends on how that exposure is completed.

## The time and fatigue contract

| Athletic component | Standard athletic booking | Compressed athletic booking |
|---|---|---|
| Prepare & Access | 0–15 min | 0–10 min |
| Explosiveness | 15–45 min | 10–35 min |
| Strength | 45–75 min | 35–55 min |
| Capacity / Competition or appropriate readiness transition | 75–90 min | 55–60 min |
| Separate Body Control / Tumbling | Additional booked 30 min; 120 min total | Additional booked 30 min; 90 min total |

The final athletic window is not a requirement to exhaust athletes before tumbling. Use suitable low-fatigue competition, useful recovery or readiness work when the later skills require it. Do not hide additional sprint contacts or strength volume there. New or consequential tumbling skills need the separate coach’s readiness decision; fatigue may require familiar foundations or a separately planned fresh-learning opportunity.

Preserve the current recognizable preparation base and its final hinge/squat observations. Derive the two specific drills from the day’s actual Explosiveness and Strength demands: one position/movement rehearsal and one progressive rehearsal. The compact base already described in the philosophy is the starting approach for the 10-minute version. All age variants, demonstrations, queues, return travel, water and transitions must later be timed; this stage does not claim the complete group preparation has been proven to fit.

Use the 15-athlete planning group and the owner's confirmed **three 12 m lanes**. Fit movement and stopping inside the available length unless additional usable run-off is established. Lane widths, outside returns, adjacent floor space, coach count and exact equipment quantities remain unresolved. Available equipment categories are recorded in the [shared facility reference](../FACILITY_AND_EQUIPMENT.md); use them when they serve the selected objective. Initial feasibility work will test **two qualified athletic coaches** with all active work visible, familiar variations and no simultaneous unfamiliar high-consequence tasks. This is a conditional planning assumption, not a staffing recommendation or proof of adequate coverage. If actual demands cannot be supervised, simplify or serialize them and recalculate; do not silently assume more staff. The separate tumbling program needs its own confirmed staffing, space and prerequisites.

No maximal-velocity prescription is final until the available buildup, running distance and safe run-off are verified. A short acceleration is a different stimulus and must be labeled that way. Exact equipment quantities and substitutions are resolved before session finalization.
